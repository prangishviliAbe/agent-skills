# Copy as design

Read when writing or reviewing headlines, calls to action, feature descriptions, proof, and microcopy. Generic wording is the fastest tell of generic design, and unsupported claims are a trust and honesty problem.

## Make each line specific

Ask of every headline and description: who exactly, doing what, with which result, in which mechanism? If the line would fit any competitor, it has no information yet.

| Generic | Question to answer | Specific direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Streamline your workflow" | Which workflow, for whom, which step disappears? | "Turn a supplier email into a purchase order in one click." |
| "Unlock the power of data" | What data, what decision? | "See which of 400 SKUs will run out before Friday." |
| "Seamless integration" | With what, how long? | "Connects to Shopify and Xero in about five minutes." (only if true) |
| "Built for the future" | What does it do today? | Describe the capability |
| "Trusted by thousands" | Which customers, how many, verified? | Real names with permission, a real number with its source, or nothing |

These phrases are prompts to be specific, not banned words: a phrase that is literally true and informative can stay.

## Headline and call-to-action patterns

- Lead with the outcome for the reader, then the mechanism. One idea per headline. Avoid the rhythm tic of three short fragments ("Fast. Simple. Secure.") unless each word is a distinct, proven claim.
- Label actions by what happens next ("Start free trial", "Get the price list", "Book a 20-minute call"). "Get started" is fine when the next step is obvious.
- Put the secondary action in a quieter style and make it a real alternative, not filler.

## Proof and honesty

- Use evidence you actually have: real customer quotes with consent, named case studies, measured results with a date and source, real screenshots of the real product.
- If evidence is missing, say less or mark the slot as a needed asset. Never invent testimonials, logos, user counts, ratings, uptime figures, or screenshots, and never present sample data as real.
- Conceptual imagery and illustrative charts are fine when labeled as such.

## Voice and rhythm

Pick a voice from the audience and stay with it: precise for engineers, warm for consumers, calm for finance and health. Use active voice and concrete nouns; vary sentence length; cut intensifiers and stacked adjectives. Read the page aloud: repeated sentence shapes and em-dash asides in every paragraph sound machine-written.

## Microcopy and structure

Name sections by their content ("Pricing", "How the audit works"), not by decoration. Avoid tiny uppercase "eyebrow" labels above every heading unless they carry navigation or categorization the reader uses. Buttons, errors, and empty states follow plain-language rules (the UX copy guidance applies equally here: verb plus object, say what happened and what to do).

## Bilingual and Georgian copy

Write each language natively, not as word-for-word translation of the other, and keep the claims identical in substance. Georgian often needs more characters than English for the same idea; plan line breaks and button widths with real strings. Decide formal or informal address once with the brand. Treat any machine or unreviewed translation as a draft until a fluent speaker reviews it, and say so.

## Review checklist

- Would this headline survive a competitor swap?
- Is every number, quote, and logo real and attributable?
- Does each button say what happens next?
- Are two languages equivalent in meaning, with layout tested on the longer one?
- Did you read it aloud and cut the filler?
