# Finding a visual thesis

Read when the direction is undefined, when the work risks defaulting to a generic aesthetic, or when a client says "modern and clean".

## What a thesis is

One sentence that states why *this* product looks the way it does, tied to something true about it.

```
Because <something specific and true about this product, audience, or domain>,
the interface <does this concrete visual thing>,
which shows up as <2–3 recurring anchors>.
```

A real one:

> Because this is a working tool for archivists who read dense Georgian manuscripts all day, the interface stays quiet and paper-like, which shows up as a warm off-white canvas, hairline rules instead of cards, and a single ink-blue accent used only for actions.

A fake one:

> A modern, clean, premium design with smooth animations and a professional feel.

The second is a mood, and moods produce defaults. The first constrains every later decision, which is what you actually want from a direction.

## Where to look for the source

Interrogate the product until something specific appears. Any of these is enough:

| Source | Question | Example outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material world | What physical objects, tools, or environments surround this work? | A logistics tool borrowing the density and legibility of shipping manifests |
| Craft and process | How is the thing actually made? | A bakery site with the rhythm of a folded, hand-set menu |
| History | What visual language does this field already own? | A legal archive using the proportions of case reporters and marginalia |
| Constraint | What is genuinely hard here? | A field app for outdoor work: enormous targets, extreme contrast, glove-friendly spacing |
| Data shape | What does the content actually look like? | A product with long, variable-length names designing around wrapping, not truncation |
| Audience ritual | What does the user do right before and after? | A booking flow designed for someone standing in a queue, one-handed |
| Language | What script and register does the content live in? | A Georgian-first interface designed around its vertical metrics rather than Latin defaults |

## Turn the thesis into anchors

A thesis needs two or three recurring, recognizable devices. More than three is noise; fewer is not a direction.

Choose one from each of at least two groups:

- **Composition:** a dominant left edge, a two-column asymmetry, a deliberate overlap, a fixed rail, a modular grid with intentional breaks
- **Typography:** a distinctive display face against a neutral text face, a very large step between roles, all-lowercase labels, numerals treated as a feature
- **Surface:** paper-like warm neutrals, hairline rules, a single tonal step, a strong border language, a dark editorial canvas
- **Image treatment:** a consistent crop ratio, a duotone or grade, a consistent margin, a recurring subject rule
- **Detail:** one signature radius, a corner rule, an underline behavior, a recurring marker or index numbering

Then apply them consistently. Recognition comes from repetition, not from variety.

## Direction families, and how each fails

Every direction has a specific failure mode. Knowing it in advance is what separates execution from imitation.

| Direction | Does well | Fails as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Editorial / quiet | Long-form reading, credibility, archives, research | Under-differentiated and timid; every project looks the same |
| Utility / dense | Professional tools, admin, data work | Cramped and unwelcoming; ignores first-time users |
| Expressive / maximalist | Culture, events, food, fashion, portfolios | Illegible, and the noise buries the primary action |
| Technical / precise | Developer tools, infrastructure, hardware | Cold and monotone; nothing carries emphasis |
| Warm / human | Health, education, community, local services | Sentimental and vague; decoration replaces information |
| Brutalist / raw | Statement work, counter-positioning | Hostile by accident, and inaccessible |

Choose the family the brief actually needs, then guard against its specific failure — not against a general fear of "too much".

## When references are provided

Extract **principles**, never assets. Transferable: density, contrast ratio between roles, spacing rhythm, image crop discipline, the proportion of white space, the number of colors used, the tempo of motion.

Not transferable: a distinctive layout signature, trade dress, a proprietary illustration style, a recognizable typeface pairing that identifies the brand, or a signature interaction.

Say what you took and why. "Their density and their two-color discipline, not their logo geometry."

## When no direction is given

Do not default to the same restrained editorial look every time — that is its own kind of slop, just quieter. Run three quick options against the brief:

1. The **conventional** one the audience already expects
2. The **content-derived** one from the strongest source above
3. The **counter-positioned** one that deliberately looks unlike this category

Pick one, say why, and name what you rejected. A stated choice reads as design; an unstated one reads as a default.

## Testing the thesis

- Can you say it in one sentence without the words modern, clean, sleek, or premium?
- Does it make at least two concrete decisions *for* you (a color rule, a type rule, a spacing rule)?
- Would violating it be noticeable on the page?
- Would the design look wrong on a competitor's product?

If any answer is no, the thesis is still a mood. Go back to the source table.
